Mini-VLA Push Lab (v2)

A vision–language–action (VLA) model is a robot policy that takes what it sees and what it was told and outputs motor commands. Think of it as a chauffeur: the eyes read the road, the ears take the passenger's “drop me at the corner”, and the hands turn that into steering, many times a second. This lab runs your three trained networks (policy.pt, policy_bc_large.pt, policy_dagger.pt, decoded from the checkpoints into JavaScript) on a browser copy of your MuJoCo push environment. It also adds a toy language layer, so you can watch a full instruction → goal → state → action loop and see where behavioral cloning breaks and DAgger recovers. The second tab places this mini-VLA next to real VLA architectures such as RT-2, OpenVLA and π0.

Benchmark in the browser

Baseline and wide runs use the exact 500 test starts from robustness.py --seed 2026, exported from NumPy, so the browser numbers can be compared one-to-one with your CSV. The browser physics is a 2-D re-implementation of the MuJoCo soft contact (impedance 0.95, damping 105 s⁻¹, stiffness 2632 s⁻², friction 1). In a Python check it matched MuJoCo to within 0.4 points on every policy. The noise scenario uses a different random stream from NumPy, so expect agreement within about ±1 point there.

The two ideas in one line each
Behavioral cloning:  θ* = arg minθ  𝔼(s,a) ~ dπE ‖ πθ(s) − a ‖²2
DAgger:  𝒟k+1 = 𝒟k ∪ { (s, πE(s)) : s ~ dπk }

BC learns from states the expert visits (dπ_E). Once the clone drifts off that path, its small errors compound, and the expected cost can grow like O(εT²) over a horizon T. DAgger relabels the states the learner visits (dπ_k), which brings the bound back to O(εT). Analogy: BC is learning to drive by watching dashcam footage of a perfect driver. DAgger puts you in the driver's seat with an instructor who says what they would do at every moment, including the moments after you have already drifted.

Real VLAs share one blueprint. A pretrained vision-language model (VLM) reads camera patches and the instruction as one token sequence. An action head then turns its hidden state into motor commands. Click any block to see what it does in a real VLA and what plays that role in your lab.

② Discrete action tokens · RT-2 / OpenVLA

These are the live outputs of the selected net in the lab. A value near 0 on both axes means the policy has stalled, which BC often does on out-of-distribution goals. Each action dimension in [−1, 1] is cut into 256 bins. The bin index becomes an ordinary vocabulary token, so the VLM “writes” actions like words, autoregressively. The rounding error is at most 1/255 ≈ 0.004 per dimension. Your MLP skips this step and regresses the two floats directly.

③ Flow-matching action chunk · π0
0.00

π0 predicts a whole chunk of H future actions at once. It starts from Gaussian noise A⁰ ~ 𝒩(0, I) and integrates a learned velocity field vθ(Aτ, o) from τ = 0 to 1 in about 10 Euler steps. Here the target chunk is the next H = 20 pusher waypoints of the expert from the current lab state. The path is the linear interpolant Aτ = (1−τ)A⁰ + τA, along which the ideal field is A − A⁰.

How to use the Mini-VLA Push Lab

Quick start

  1. Step 1 · Set up. Choose a policy (who drives), a scenario (how starts are drawn) and a start position. The three presets are good first picks: Nominal, BC fails, DAgger succeeds and Expert blind spot.
  2. Step 2 · Goal (optional). Type an instruction or tap an example chip, then press Set goal. If you skip this, the goal comes from the test start.
  3. Step 3 · Run. Press ▶ Run. Use Step for one 0.05 s decision at a time, and Replay to rerun the same start.
  4. Change one setting (for example the policy) and press Run again to compare on the identical start.

Buttons

  • ▶ Run / ❚❚ Pause / ↺ Run again: start, pause, or rerun a finished episode.
  • Replay: back to t = 0 with the same start and goal, then run.
  • Random start: draw one of the 500 paired test starts. test start # picks one by number.
  • RESET: restore every setting to its default (DAgger, baseline, start #0, speed 1×).
  • Drag the puck, goal or pusher on the canvas to edit the start. The episode returns to t = 0 and waits for Run.
  • An instruction given while running re-targets the robot live, as a real VLA would on a new command.

Display options

  • expert label: grey dashed arrow showing what the scripted expert would do in the current state.
  • ghosts: puck paths of the other learned policies from the same start.
  • action field: the policy's action at every pusher position, for the current puck and goal.
  • keep pushing 15 steps after success: the benchmark stops at the first moment the puck is within 9 cm, which can look like a near miss. This option lets you watch the puck settle.

What you are looking at

Pusher: the robot, a 5.5 cm sphere moved by the action a ∈ [−1, 1]² × 0.05 s. Puck: the object, a damped sphere on x/y slide joints. Goal: success when ‖puck − goal‖ < 9 cm (dashed ring), within 110 steps. Dashed boxes: where training data came from (puck starts, goals).

Background

The task is your MuJoCo push experiment. A hand-written expert goes behind the puck and pushes it toward the goal. BC (behavioral cloning) copies the expert from 6.7k state/action pairs, and Large BC from 39.9k. DAgger lets the learner drive, asks the expert to label the states it actually visits, and retrains.

On baseline starts every learner scores 100%. On wide starts the clones leave the region they were trained on, small errors compound, and BC falls to 34.8% (Large BC 43.2%). DAgger reaches 81.4%. More data alone does not fix this. Data in the right places does.

The expert has one blind spot. When the goal is within about 50° of straight up from the puck (dy > 0.642), its upward detour point lands on the puck, so it rams it away. DAgger learns from that expert and inherits the flaw. The red expert blind spot badge appears whenever this geometry occurs.

The language layer is a rule-based parser, not a learned model. It recognises corners, directions with amounts (“10 cm left”), “center”, “toward the robot”, “back where it started” and coordinates such as “(0.3, −0.2)”. The policies themselves are your trained networks, running in the browser.

Scenarios: noise adds 1.5 cm Gaussian error to the positions the policy sees (dashed ghost puck). Perturb moves the puck 12 cm upward at step 25, though most nominal episodes finish before then.
